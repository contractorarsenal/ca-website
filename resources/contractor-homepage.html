What Should Be on a Contractor Homepage?

Your homepage has one job: help the right customer understand what you do, trust your company, and know what to do next.

The Job of Your Homepage

Someone lands on your homepage because they need work done and they're deciding who to call. They don't want your life story. They want to know fast: is this the right company, do they work near me, and can I reach them right now.

If your homepage answers those three questions in the first few seconds, it's doing its job. If it doesn't, the visitor leaves and calls the next result.

What Customers Need to See

Before anything else, a visitor needs a plain, direct description of what you do. Not a slogan. Not "quality craftsmanship since 1998." Just the trade and the work, in words a homeowner would use.

Bad: "Delivering excellence in residential solutions."

Good: "Roof repair and replacement for homes across Seattle."

Services

List your main services clearly, near the top of the page. If you do five things, name all five. A customer who does storm damage repair shouldn't have to guess whether you handle that or just re-roofs.

  • Use the words customers actually search, not internal trade jargon
  • Link each service to its own page if you have one
  • Keep the list short enough to scan in five seconds

Trust

Once someone knows what you do, they're deciding whether to trust you with their house or their money. This is where reviews, real project photos, and licensing or insurance information earn their place.

  • A star rating with a review count, not just "5 stars" with no source
  • Real photos of real completed work, not stock photography
  • Licensing and insurance details where your trade requires them

Location

Say where you work. Not just your city, your actual service area. If someone in a neighboring town can't tell whether you'll take the job, they'll call someone who makes it obvious.

Contact

Make the next step obvious: a visible phone number they can tap on mobile, and a short estimate form for people who'd rather not call yet. Both should be reachable without scrolling far.

Most contractor traffic is on a phone. If your call button is small, buried, or requires zooming in, you're losing jobs to a competitor whose site makes calling effortless.

What to Avoid

  • Long paragraphs of generic marketing copy before any real information
  • Stock photos that could belong to any company in any city
  • No phone number above the fold on mobile
  • Services buried in a menu instead of visible on the homepage

Your Homepage Should Answer the Customer's Next Question

Every section of your homepage should remove a reason for the customer to leave. What do you do. Where do you work. Can I trust you. How do I reach you. Answer those four, in that order, and the rest of the page can stay simple.
